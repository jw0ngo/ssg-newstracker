Situations group the stories of one deal, round or process across the weeks it runs, by the company they are about (read from the headline; a known portco or target wins) and by shared words, so twenty headlines on one takeover read as one card. The phase is the furthest any report got: Reported (explores, weighs, in talks), In process (advisers hired, data room, bids, shortlist, an unaccepted offer), Agreed, Completed. The heat is the hottest story's. A card is shown when a situation has more than one story or is in process; tap it for every story.

Priority is ordered by Heat, a 0–100 score for the Singapore SSG team: APAC relevance (25 — Singapore and SE Asia first, then India and ANZ, then North Asia), live process (20), technology intensity (20), sponsor relationship (15), deal size (10) and ten for the day it breaks; from then on the whole number fades with age — full for two days, two-thirds at a week, half at ten days — so the list always leads with the latest. Tap the flame number for the breakdown. An item is on the list when it is relevant: an APAC item whenever it carries an event or a focus area; a global item only when it is live or signed and in a focus area, or a fund close, a new platform or a senior hire. The rest is kept behind the link at the bottom; the page carries the last 90 days and the archive in the repo keeps everything. Event, thesis and APAC are read from the headline by keyword; the matched words sit under each tag. A portfolio company buying something is an add-on and is listed here with the buyer's name, whether or not the headline names the firm. A story tagged BACKGROUND is a profile page or a re-told old deal (the text, the url or the holding date gives it away) and sits below the bar. Pursue, Watch, Dismiss and notes are saved and shared with anyone who can open this page.

Portcos are the firm's current portfolio companies that matter to SSG: focus-area companies first, APAC-weighted, and within that the longest held at the top, because four or more years is where a sponsor starts planning the exit. The hold clock on each card fills to seven years and turns hot past four, the exit window; a story about a portco in the window scores extra Heat. A reported sale or IPO process is tagged on the card. Tap a card for the business in a sentence and its five latest headlines, which are the hand-kept signals plus a daily news search on the company's own name. Valuations are estimates with their basis beside them.

The dashboard reads the same roster as the boards: the sponsors' portfolio companies in Asia-Pacific outside India and mainland China. Thematic investing counts each company once, under its SSG sector board. Vintage is the year the sponsor first invested, from the hold date on file; a company with no dated entry is counted beside the panel. Bolt-ons are acquisitions made by the portfolio companies themselves, read from the headline archive: a headline naming the company as the buyer at signed or closed stage, several reports of one deal folded into one event. The archive has carried every feed story since October 2026 and was backfilled before that, so earlier months understate activity. The sponsor chips narrow every chart to one sponsor; tap a heatmap cell for the companies behind it.

Each board is every sponsor's portfolio company in one SSG focus area, and the All board is the whole roster with each card tagged by focus area, in Asia-Pacific outside India and mainland China, in a column per country: the HQ country, or for a company based elsewhere the first board market it operates in. Cards are ordered by time held, longest at the top, because the hold clock is the exit clock: four or more years is where a sponsor starts planning the exit. The hold bar shows one block per year held, the last one lighter for a part year, and the bar, the hold figure and the card's left edge run from grey for a new holding to red at eight years or more. A company with no hold date on file sits at the bottom of its column. A reported sale or IPO process, reported talks, a partial exit or a listing with the sponsor still holding is tagged on the card. The owner tag names the sponsor, and a sponsor chip narrows the board to one. Tap a card for the business in a sentence, its exit status and its latest headlines.

Targets are Jobi's candidates, not anything Warburg Pincus has said: companies in a focus area that look like what the firm buys, with the signals that make the case. A live process is reported by the press; everything else is a watch. Priority puts sponsor-owned companies first, longest hold at the top: a fund four or more years in is the seller most likely to run a process.
